Climate Science Context Guide

Understanding how true observations can lead to misleading conclusions

1. The Water Vapor Trap

The Claim: "Global warming is caused by water vapor, not humans, because water vapor is the most abundant greenhouse gas."

What's True
Water vapor is the most abundant greenhouse gas and accounts for a large share of Earth's natural greenhouse effect.
The Scientific Context
Water vapor primarily acts as an amplifier, or feedback, rather than the initial driver of today's warming. Long-lived greenhouse gases such as CO₂ raise temperatures; warmer air then contains more water vapor, which amplifies the initial warming. NASA estimates that water-vapor feedback more than doubles the warming caused by CO₂ alone.

2. The Ice Sheet Paradox

The Claim: "Ice sheets are actually growing larger, meaning the planet isn't really warming."

What's True
Some parts of East Antarctica have experienced localized mass gains during certain periods because snowfall added ice.
The Scientific Context
Local gains do not describe the balance of the entire ice-sheet system, and no single snowfall event should automatically be attributed to climate change. Satellite measurements show that both Antarctica and Greenland are losing ice overall as the atmosphere and oceans warm. NASA's latest long-term estimates are approximately 135 billion metric tons per year from Antarctica and 264 billion metric tons per year from Greenland.

3. The Global Greening Illusion

The Claim: "The planet is becoming much greener, which proves that rising CO₂ is good for nature."

What's True
Satellites have measured increased leaf area across much of the world's vegetated land. Higher atmospheric CO₂ has contributed substantially, alongside nitrogen deposition, land-management changes, and climate-related changes in temperature, precipitation, and sunlight.
The Scientific Context
A "greener" satellite image does not, by itself, show that ecosystems or people are better off. The CO₂ fertilization effect is real, but it can diminish over time and is constrained by water, nutrients, temperature, and other stresses. Greening therefore does not negate the independently measured harms from warming, drought, extreme heat, wildfire risk, sea-level rise, and shifting ecosystems.

Source: NASA Earth Observatory on global greening and its limits.

Sources: NASA Earth Science and peer-reviewed research summarized by NASA. Measurements and estimates can be updated as new observations become available.
